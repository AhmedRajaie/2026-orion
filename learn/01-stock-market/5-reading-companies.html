01 — WHAT FUNDAMENTALS MEAN

The Lens Most Professionals Actually Use

We covered charts before companies because charts are flashier — but for long-term wealth, this is the lens that matters more. Fundamental analysis asks one core question: is the price you're paying less than what you're getting? Everything else in this session is just tools to answer that question.

"Price is what you pay. Value is what you get."
— WARREN BUFFETT
Fundamentals are everything that determines what a business is actually worth — its earnings, assets, debt, growth, management quality, competitive position. The stock price is what people are willing to pay right now. The two are related, but they're not the same — and the gap between them is where investing returns come from.
The Company
A real business doing real things
Has employees, factories, customers, contracts, debts, profits. Generates cash. Has competitors. Faces regulation. Pays taxes. The company exists whether or not you trade its stock today.
The Stock
A claim on a fraction of that company
A piece of paper (now a database entry) that gives you ownership of a tiny slice of the company. Its price moves every second. The price reflects what other investors think the company is worth — which can be very different from what the company is actually worth.
↳ The big idea
A great company at a terrible price is a bad investment. A mediocre company at a great price can be a fine one. Fundamental analysis isn't about finding the "best" company — it's about finding the biggest gap between what something is worth and what the market is currently charging for it.
02 — FINANCIAL STATEMENTS

The Three Documents That Tell You Everything

Every public company is required to publish three financial statements every quarter. Together they tell you almost everything you need to know about whether a business is healthy, growing, and worth your money. Reading them isn't accounting — it's investigation. Each one answers a different question.

STATEMENT 01

Income Statement (P&L)

Question it answers: "Did the company make money this period?"

Top line: total revenue (sales). From that, subtract everything the company spent — cost of goods sold (COGS), operating expenses, interest, taxes. The bottom line is net income: profit. The most quoted number from this statement is EPS (Earnings Per Share) — net income divided by shares outstanding.

Gross MarginGross profit ÷ revenue. Tells you how efficient the core business is at making each sale.
Operating MarginOperating income ÷ revenue. Tells you how efficient the company is overall, before financing and taxes.
Net MarginNet income ÷ revenue. The bottom-line profitability — what's left after everything.
STATEMENT 02

Balance Sheet

Question it answers: "What does the company own and owe at this exact moment?"
ASSETS
What the company OWNS
→ Cash & investments
→ Inventory
→ Receivables (money owed)
→ Property, plant, equipment
→ Intangibles (brands, IP)
=
LIABILITIES
What it OWES — debt, payables, lease obligations
EQUITY
What's left for shareholders — Assets minus Liabilities
THE FUNDAMENTAL ACCOUNTING EQUATION — ALWAYS BALANCES

A snapshot at one moment in time. The Income Statement is a movie of what happened over a quarter — the Balance Sheet is a still photo of what the company looks like on the last day of that quarter.

Debt-to-EquityHow much the company borrows vs. what shareholders own. High debt = fragile in bad years.
Current RatioShort-term assets ÷ short-term liabilities. Above 1 means the company can pay its bills this year.
Book ValueTotal equity. The accounting "worth" of the company. Often very different from market value.
STATEMENT 03

Cash Flow Statement

Question it answers: "Where did real cash actually go?"
OPERATING
Cash from running the business
Cash actually collected from customers, minus cash paid to suppliers and employees. The most important section — a healthy business should always be cash-positive here.
INVESTING
Cash spent on the future
Buying factories, equipment, acquisitions. Usually negative for growing companies — they're plowing money back in. Watch capex closely.
FINANCING
Cash from/to lenders & owners
Borrowing, repaying debt, paying dividends, issuing or buying back shares. Tells you how the company funds itself.

The most important number on this statement is Free Cash Flow — operating cash minus capital expenditures. That's the actual cash the business throws off after paying to maintain itself. Companies can fake earnings with accounting tricks, but real cash is much harder to manufacture.

↳ The single most important lesson
Profit isn't cash, and cash isn't profit. A company can show "profits" on the income statement while bleeding cash — by booking sales it hasn't been paid for yet, or capitalising costs that should be expenses. Companies almost never go bankrupt because of low profits. They go bankrupt because they run out of cash. Always check both statements together.
03 — VALUATION RATIOS

Shortcuts to Compare Companies Fast

Once you can read the statements, you have the raw materials to value a company. But you don't always have time to do it from scratch — so professionals use ratios as quick comparison shortcuts. Each ratio answers a specific question. None of them is the answer by itself.

Ratio 01
P/E Ratio
Price ÷ Earnings per Share
"How many years of current earnings am I paying for one share?" A P/E of 12 means: if earnings stayed flat, you'd recover your purchase price in 12 years.
Low P/E: cheap, or genuinely struggling.
High P/E: expensive, or growing fast enough to justify it.
Ratio 02
P/B Ratio
Price ÷ Book Value per Share
"How much am I paying per dollar of accounting equity?" Most useful for asset-heavy businesses — banks, real estate, industrials. Less useful for tech and brands where most value is intangible.
P/B < 1: trading below book value — possible bargain or warning sign.
P/B > 3: market believes the company is worth far more than its accounting books say.
Ratio 03
Dividend Yield
Annual Dividend ÷ Share Price
"What cash return am I getting just from holding this?" A dividend yield of 6% means you'd earn 6% per year in cash dividends, before any price change. Compare to bank deposit rates and bond yields.
Caution: very high yields (e.g. 15%+) often mean the market expects the dividend to be cut soon.
Ratio 04
EV/EBITDA
Enterprise Value ÷ EBITDA
A more "neutral" version of P/E. EV adjusts for debt and cash; EBITDA strips out tax and accounting noise. Useful for comparing companies with different debt levels — e.g. a heavily-leveraged real estate company vs. a debt-free tech firm.
Used heavily in M&A and private-equity valuation. Rough rule: 8–10x is "normal" for mature businesses.
Ratio 05
ROE
Net Income ÷ Equity
"For every pound of shareholder money, how much profit does the business generate?" The cleanest single measure of how good a business is at compounding capital. Buffett's favourite metric.
ROE > 15% consistently is exceptional.
ROE < 10% is mediocre.
Ratio 06
PEG Ratio
P/E ÷ Earnings Growth Rate
P/E adjusted for growth. A P/E of 30 sounds expensive — but if earnings are growing 30% per year, the PEG is 1.0, which is fair. The classic Peter Lynch rule: PEG below 1 is interesting; above 2 is expensive.
↳ A high P/E isn't always "expensive"
A P/E of 8 on a declining business is more expensive than a P/E of 25 on a business growing 30% a year. The ratio is meaningless without context: industry, growth rate, debt load, profitability trend. Ratios are starting points for questions, not answers.
04 — SECTOR LENSES

Different Businesses Need Different Lenses

A bank doesn't work like a tech company. A real estate developer doesn't work like a telecoms operator. Applying the same ratios across all sectors produces nonsense — banks always look "cheap" on P/B, tech companies always look "expensive" on P/E. Each sector has its own playbook of what to look at first.

Sector — Banks
Examples: CIB, QNB, Faisal Islamic Bank
Banks make money on the spread between what they pay depositors and what they charge borrowers. P/E is less informative — use these instead:
→ ROE — best banks compound at 18–25%
→ NIM (Net Interest Margin) — wider is better
→ NPL Ratio — non-performing loans, lower is better
→ Capital Adequacy — regulatory cushion against losses
→ P/B — most-watched valuation metric
Sector — Industrials & Materials
Examples: Ezz Steel, Sidi Kerir Petrochemicals, Cleopatra Hospitals
Cyclical, capital-heavy. Profits swing wildly with commodity prices and the broader economy. Watch:
→ Operating Margin — efficiency at converting sales to profit
→ Debt-to-Equity — capital-heavy = dangerous if leveraged
→ Capacity Utilisation — are factories running full?
→ EV/EBITDA — neutralises debt differences
Sector — Real Estate
Examples: Talaat Moustafa, Palm Hills, SODIC
Two business models: developers (build & sell) vs. landlords (build & rent). Different metrics for each:
→ NAV (Net Asset Value) — sum-of-parts land & project value
→ P/NAV — trading at a discount or premium to NAV?
→ Pre-sales backlog — units sold but not yet delivered
→ Land bank — undeveloped land at cost vs. market value
Sector — Telecoms
Examples: Telecom Egypt (Orange), Vodafone
Subscription businesses with heavy infrastructure costs. Recurring revenue, slow growth, high cash flow. The lens is:
→ ARPU (Average Revenue Per User) — going up or down?
→ Churn — % of subscribers leaving each year
→ Capex Intensity — capital spending vs. revenue
→ Free Cash Flow Yield — what % of price the business generates in cash
Sector — Consumer & Retail
Examples: Edita, Juhayna, Eastern Tobacco
Brand value, distribution, and consumer purchasing power. Sensitive to inflation, FX, and disposable income.
→ Same-store sales growth — organic vs. expansion-driven
→ Gross Margin — pricing power vs. competitors
→ Brand strength — qualitative but matters
→ Inventory turnover — how fast products move
Sector — Financial Services
Examples: EFG Hermes (EFGD), Beltone, CI Capital
Brokerages, asset managers, and investment banks. Revenue tied to market activity — volumes, IPOs, M&A pipeline.
→ Brokerage market share — how much of EGX volume
→ AUM (Assets Under Management) — recurring fee base
→ Fee income vs. principal income — quality of revenue
→ Cost-to-income ratio — operational efficiency
↳ Always compare like-for-like
When you look up a company's P/E, immediately check the average P/E for its sector. CIB at P/E 8 may be cheap for the EGX market overall but expensive vs. other Egyptian banks. The benchmark is always the peer group, not the index.
05 — THE CHECKLIST

A Beginner's Mental Framework

After all the ratios and statements and sector lenses — when you sit down with a real company, what do you actually ask? Here's a simple four-question checklist that filters out 90% of bad ideas before you even open a chart.

Question 01
Does it make money?
Open the income statement. Has the company been profitable for the last 3+ years? Is operating cash flow consistently positive? If not — why are you even looking at this?
Companies that don't make money can still be great investments — but only if you have a specific thesis for why that changes. Most retail investors don't.
Question 02
Can it survive a bad year?
Open the balance sheet. Is there cash on hand? Is debt manageable relative to earnings and equity? In Egypt specifically — does it have FX exposure that could blow up the next time the pound moves?
A great business with a fragile balance sheet can go bankrupt in a single bad year. Survival comes before growth.
Question 03
Is the price reasonable?
Compare its valuation ratios (P/E, P/B, EV/EBITDA, dividend yield) to the sector average and to its own history. Is it more expensive than usual? Cheaper? Why?
"Reasonable" doesn't mean "cheap." Sometimes the answer is "yes, this is fairly priced for a great business" — that's still investable.
Question 04
Do I trust management?
Read the latest annual report — specifically the management letter. Are they straight with shareholders about challenges? Have they done what they said they'd do over the past few years? Do they own meaningful equity themselves?
The numbers are made by people. If management isn't trustworthy, the numbers can't be either.
↳ Why retail investors really lose
Most retail investors don't lose because they pick fundamentally bad companies. They lose because they:
→ Pay too much for good companies (buying after the story is already in the price)
→ Sell good companies too early (taking small wins, missing the compounding)
→ Average down on bad companies (turning a small loss into a catastrophic one)
→ Confuse a falling stock price with a falling company (and panic-sell at the bottom)
Fundamentals don't make those mistakes go away. But knowing them helps. When the price drops 30% on a company you fundamentally understand, you can decide whether the business actually changed — or whether the market is just panicking. That's the difference between buying when the price is right and selling when the price is wrong.
