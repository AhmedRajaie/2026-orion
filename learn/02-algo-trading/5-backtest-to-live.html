From Backtest
to Live

The gap between “it worked on paper” and “it is running with real money.”

01 — WHY THIS MATTERS

Paper Is Clean. Markets Are Not.

Part 4 showed how to test an idea on historical data. Part 5 is about what changes when the idea starts touching the real market.

A backtest lives in a clean world. Prices are already recorded. The data is sitting still. Orders either happen according to your assumptions or they do not. Nothing disconnects. Nothing freezes. No broker API returns a confusing error. No order gets partially filled at an awkward price.

Live trading is different. The strategy is no longer just a set of rules. It becomes a small operating system: reading data, making decisions, placing orders, tracking positions, enforcing limits, and knowing when to stop.

Going live is not just “turning the backtest on.”

It is the moment your strategy needs execution, monitoring, and risk controls.
02 — THE GAP

What Changes When Money Is Real

The rules may be the same. The environment is not.

Backtest World

Historical prices are fixed. Orders are simulated. Costs are modeled. Mistakes cost nothing. You can run the same period again and again.

Live Market

Prices move while you act. Orders can miss, fill partially, or fill badly. Systems can disconnect. Mistakes cost real money and cannot be replayed.

That does not mean live trading should feel mysterious. It means you need to name the moving parts. Once you name them, you can design around them.

03 — EXECUTION

Order Types Revisited

You already know the basic order types. In algo trading, the question becomes: which order best matches the assumptions your strategy was tested under?

Market Order
Execution certainty
You are likely to get filled, but you give up price control. In fast or illiquid markets, the fill can be worse than expected.
Limit Order
Price certainty
You control the worst acceptable price, but the trade may not happen if the market does not reach your limit.
Stop Order
Conditional action
Used to trigger exits or entries when price crosses a level. Useful, but still exposed to gaps and execution quality.
Timed Order
Execution schedule
Splits execution across time. Useful when a large order could move the market or attract attention.

There is no universally “best” order type. The right choice depends on the strategy. A fast breakout strategy may care more about getting filled. A mean-reversion strategy may care more about price discipline. The point is to make the live execution match the tested assumption as closely as possible.

If your backtest assumes price discipline, but your live system uses aggressive market orders, you are no longer trading the same strategy.
04 — OPERATIONS

The Boring Things That Matter

Most live algo failures are not dramatic. They are boring system problems that were not handled clearly.

O1

Data Feed Problems

If the price feed disconnects, becomes stale, or sends bad data, the algo can make decisions on the wrong picture of the market. A live system needs to know when data is fresh, when it is stale, and when to stop acting.

O2

Broker API Errors

Orders can be rejected. Requests can time out. An API can return an error after the order actually reached the broker. Your system must be able to check the real order state instead of guessing.

O3

Partial Fills

You may ask to buy 10,000 shares and receive only 3,000. The strategy now has a position, but not the position it expected. Live code needs to handle partial fills as a normal case, not an exception.

O4

Duplicate Orders

A retry mechanism can accidentally submit the same order twice if it does not check whether the first order succeeded. Good systems are paranoid about duplicates because they quietly turn a normal position into an oversized one.

↳ This is why your first live algo should be boring. Boring is easier to monitor, easier to debug, and easier to trust.

  • Max position size: the largest amount of capital the algo can put into one trade.
  • Max total exposure: the largest percentage of the account that can be deployed at once.
  • Max daily loss: the point where the system stops trading for the day, even if new signals appear.
  • Drawdown threshold: the point where the strategy is paused and reviewed instead of blindly continuing.
  • Kill switch: a simple way to shut the system down immediately. Always.
The risk controls must outrank the strategy.

If the strategy says “buy” and the risk layer says “stop,” the answer is stop.
06 — WHEN TO PAUSE

When Not to Trust Your Algo

A live strategy needs monitoring because a strategy can stop working without throwing an error.

P1

Regime Changes

A strategy built on one market environment may struggle in another. Volatility changes. Interest rates change. Liquidity changes. The same rules can behave differently when the market’s personality shifts.

P2

Drawdown Thresholds

Drawdown is not just something to observe. It is something to predefine. If the strategy falls beyond the level you said was acceptable, the system should pause. Not after a debate. Not after “one more trade.” Pause first, analyze second.

P3

Alpha Decay

Every strategy faces the slow possibility of decay. Once an inefficiency is widely known and traded, the edge erodes. The market does not adapt consciously, but the population of traders does. The result looks the same: your returns degrade.


This does not mean the algo is broken in the technical sense. It may still run perfectly. It may still enter and exit exactly as designed. The problem is that the opportunity it used to exploit is no longer paying the same way.

P4

Behavior Drift

If the live strategy starts behaving very differently from the backtest — more trades, longer holds, worse fills, deeper drawdowns — that difference is information. Do not ignore it because the code is still running.

↳ Monitoring rolling performance matters as much as the initial backtest. The question is not “did this strategy work once?” It is “is it still behaving like the strategy I tested?”
07 — HUMAN ROLE

Knowing When to Override Is a Skill

Automation removes emotional hesitation from execution. It does not remove human responsibility from supervision.

A beginner mistake is thinking that once a system is automated, touching it means you failed. That is not true. A live algo is not a religion. It is a tool. Tools are monitored, paused, repaired, and sometimes retired.

The skill is not overriding because you are scared. The skill is overriding because a pre-defined condition says the system should not continue: data is stale, drawdown has breached the limit, fills are abnormal, the broker is rejecting orders, or the strategy’s live behavior no longer matches what you tested.

The algo does not know it is broken.

You design the conditions that tell it when to stop.
08 — THE RIGHT SEQUENCE

The First Live Algo Should Be Boring

Boring does not mean weak. Boring means understandable enough to supervise.

Start with a simple strategy. Small size. Clear logs. Conservative costs. No leverage. No complicated AI layer. No hidden decision logic that you cannot explain. The goal of the first live system is not to impress anyone. The goal is to learn how live execution actually behaves.

Once you can safely run something boring, monitor it, pause it, and understand its failures, you have earned the right to make it more sophisticated.

↳ Good live trading is not dramatic. It is controlled.
09 — SUMMARY

What You Should Now Understand

Six things to carry into system architecture.

Key Takeaways — Part 05
  • Going live is not just turning on a backtest. A live algo needs execution, monitoring, and risk controls.
  • Order types matter because live execution should match the assumptions your strategy was tested under.
  • Operational problems — stale data, API errors, partial fills, duplicate orders — are normal live-system concerns.
  • Every live algo needs real-money controls: max position size, max exposure, max daily loss, drawdown thresholds, and a kill switch.
  • Strategies can stop working because of regime changes, drawdowns, alpha decay, or behavior drift.
  • The first live algo should be boring, small, observable, and easy to shut down.
