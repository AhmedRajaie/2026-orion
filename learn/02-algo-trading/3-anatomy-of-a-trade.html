Anatomy of a Trade
and a Strategy

A 10-minute read on what every trade must define — and what makes a strategy out of trades.

01 — WHY THIS MATTERS

Why This Part Matters

Part 1 said an algo is a defined set of rules. Part 2 made the case for using them. This part gets specific about what those rules actually are.

There's a discipline an algo forces on you that manual trading lets you skip: every trade must be fully defined before it happens. No vague intentions, no "I'll figure out where to sell when I get there." If any piece is missing, the algo can't execute. That constraint isn't a limitation — it's the most useful thing about algorithmic thinking, even if you never automate anything.

02 — TRADE ATTRIBUTES

A Trade Has Four Required Attributes

Every trade — every single one, manual or automated — is defined by exactly four things.

1. Entry

When do you get in? At what price, under what conditions, triggered by what signal.

"Buy CIB at 80" is an entry. So is "Buy CIB when its 20-day moving average crosses above its 50-day moving average." The form varies. The requirement doesn't.

2. Exit / Take-Profit

At what price do you take the win? Getting out is what determines whether the trade made money.

"Sell at 90" is an exit. So is "Sell when the price is 12% above entry."

3. Stop-Loss

At what price do you cut the loss? The stop-loss isn't optional.

A trade without a defined loss exit is a trade that can take down your account.

4. Duration

How long can the trade live before you're out regardless? Time itself is a signal.

"Hold for max 30 days" and "exit by Friday close" are duration rules.

03 — THE RULE

All Four Are Required

If any one of these four is undefined, it is not a strategy yet — it is an intention.

If any one of these four is undefined, it's not a strategy yet — it's an intention.

This is the discipline algos force on you. You can't backtest "buy when it looks good." You can't backtest "hold until I feel like selling." The computer needs all four slots filled with concrete, testable values.

The slots themselves can be dynamic. A trailing stop-loss — one that rises as the trade moves in your favor — is still a defined stop, it just moves. A volatility-adjusted take-profit that scales with how much the stock typically moves is still defined. The shape can be sophisticated. The requirement is that the slot exists and has rules governing it.

If you can't articulate all four for any trade you're considering, you don't have a trade yet. You have a feeling. That distinction will save you a lot of money over time.

04 — AI OVERRIDES

Aside: What if AI Closes a Trade on Breaking News?

AI-driven event overrides are real in live trading, but they are not the rules-based foundation you can backtest.

Yes — in live trading, AI-driven event overrides are real. A system that reads news and closes a position before the stop-loss triggers can absolutely outperform a system that doesn't. This is one of the things AI brings to algo trading, and we'll cover it in Part 7.

↳ AI overrides are a layer added on top of a rules-based foundation. They are augmentation, not foundation.

These overrides sit outside the four backtestable attributes. You can't reliably backtest "what would my algo have done if it had read the news," because you don't have granular historical news data, and AI behavior on edge cases is not deterministic.

05 — STRATEGY COMPONENTS

From Trades to Strategies

A trade is a single event with four attributes. A strategy is the system that generates trades.

A strategy is the engine that decides when each trade should happen, how big it should be, and how it relates to other trades.

Signal
What condition triggers action? The signal is the hypothesis: momentum, mean reversion, events, or statistical relationships.
Entry Rule
Given the signal, when and how do you enter? Market order, limit order, acceptable price, and how long the entry stays valid.
Exit Rule
When do you leave? Profit target, stop-loss, trailing stop, signal reversal, or time-based exit.
Position Sizing
How much do you commit? Fixed amount, fixed percentage, volatility-adjusted sizing, or Kelly-style sizing.
A great signal with bad position sizing can lose money. A mediocre signal with disciplined sizing can make money.
06 — WORKED EXAMPLE

Worked Example: A Complete Strategy

The classic 20/50 moving average crossover on EGX stocks.

Signal
Universe: EGX stocks with price > EGP 5 and 30-day average daily volume > 100,000 shares. Trigger: 20-day moving average crosses above the 50-day moving average.
Entry Rule
Buy at the open of the next trading day, using a limit order at the previous day's close. If the limit doesn't fill within 30 minutes, abandon the entry.
Exit Rule
Take-profit when the 20-day moving average crosses back below the 50-day. Stop-loss 8% below entry, trailing. Max duration: 60 trading days.
Position Sizing
2% of current portfolio capital per position. Maximum 10 concurrent positions.
↳ That is a complete strategy: backtestable, executable, and specific enough that two people coding it should produce nearly identical results.
07 — RISK MANAGEMENT

Aside: Risk and Portfolio Management

Risk management and portfolio management apply to all trading — manual and algorithmic.

  • Position sizing as a percentage of capital
  • Diversification across uncorrelated positions
  • Drawdown limits — the maximum loss you will tolerate before stopping
  • Exposure caps — the maximum percentage of capital deployed at once

These are not algo-specific tools. They are trading fundamentals. Beginners often think of them as "advanced" topics for after they figure out entries and exits. They are not. They are the foundation that makes the entries and exits survivable.

An algo does not make risk management more important — it makes it more visible. When you code a strategy, you are forced to write the sizing rule explicitly, which means you confront it.

08 — SUMMARY

What You Should Now Understand

Six things to take away before moving on.

Key Takeaways — Part 03
  • Every trade is fully defined by four attributes: entry, exit/take-profit, stop-loss, and duration.
  • If any of the four is missing, it is not a strategy — it is an intention.
  • The slots can be dynamic — trailing stops and volatility-adjusted exits count — but they must exist.
  • A strategy is the engine that generates trades, built from four components: signal, entry rule, exit rule, and position sizing.
  • AI-driven event overrides are real in live trading but live outside the backtestable rule set. They are augmentation, not foundation.
  • Risk and portfolio management apply to all trading. They are foundational, not advanced.
