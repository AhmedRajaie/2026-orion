Backtesting:
Testing Ideas on the Past

How to use history as a simulator — and how to read the results without fooling yourself.

01 — WHY THIS MATTERS

From Defined Strategy to Testable Idea

Part 3 showed how to define a trade and a strategy. Now comes the next question: would this idea have worked before?

That question is the reason backtesting exists. Instead of risking real money on a strategy because it sounds smart, you run the rules through historical market data and watch what would have happened. Every entry, every exit, every stop-loss, every cost — simulated before a single live order is placed.

A backtest is not a promise that a strategy will make money. It is a way to ask a better question: is this idea coherent enough to test more seriously?

Think of it like a flight simulator. It does not prove you can fly through every storm. But it lets you practice the controls, stress the system, and discover obvious mistakes before the real aircraft leaves the ground.

02 — THE SIMULATOR

What a Backtest Actually Does

A backtest plays market history forward, one step at a time, while your strategy reacts according to its rules.

A simple backtest might ask: whenever the 20-day moving average crosses above the 50-day moving average, buy the stock. Then sell when the reverse crossover happens, or when the stop-loss is hit, or when the maximum duration expires.

The backtester walks through the historical candles and records what the strategy would have done at each point in time. The output is not just one final profit number. It is the full behavior of the strategy: when it entered, when it exited, how long trades lasted, how deep losses got, how often it traded, and whether the idea survived realistic costs.

03 — MEASUREMENT

What You Measure

A beginner looks only at return. A builder looks at the shape of the return.

Return
Did the strategy make money?
Useful, but not enough by itself. A high return with terrifying losses may not be tradable.
Win Rate
How often did it win?
A low win rate can still work if winners are much larger than losers. A high win rate can still fail if losses are huge.
Payoff
Average win vs average loss
This tells you whether the strategy makes small frequent gains, rare large gains, or something in between.
Max Drawdown
How bad did it get?
The deepest peak-to-trough loss. This is often the number that decides whether a human can actually stick with the strategy.
Trade Count
How many trades happened?
A strategy tested on 12 trades tells you much less than a strategy tested on hundreds of trades.
Exposure
How often was capital at work?
Two strategies with the same return can have very different risk if one is invested constantly and the other is rarely exposed.
↳ Backtesting is not about finding the biggest number. It is about understanding the behavior of the strategy.
04 — DATA DISCIPLINE

Treat Historical Data Like ML Data

Do not judge a strategy only on the same data you used to invent or tune it.

This is the easiest place to fool yourself. You test an idea, tweak it, test again, tweak again, and eventually the strategy looks great on the exact historical period you kept staring at. But that does not mean you found a real edge. It may only mean you memorized the past.

Example Data Split · 8 Years of History

6 Years

Untouched Holdout
1 Year
Keep this completely unseen until the end. This is where excuses go to die.

Some practitioners use a simple two-way split: train on six years, test on two. Others use three sets: train, optimize, final validation. The exact split can vary. The principle does not: keep a portion of the data untouched until the end.

For trading, the untouched portion should usually be recent. Markets evolve. A strategy that only works in old conditions may look beautiful in the past and irrelevant today. Recent data is closer to the market you are actually about to trade.

05 — COSTS

Costs: Known and Unknown

A backtest that ignores trading costs is not optimistic. It is incomplete.

Commission Is Known

Build it in exactly. If a trade has a broker commission, exchange fee, tax, or any other known cost, the backtest should subtract it every time the simulated trade happens.


There is no need to guess here. Commission is part of the trade. Ignoring it makes the result wrong by design.

Slippage Is Uncertain

Model it conservatively. Slippage is the gap between the price your backtest assumes and the price you actually get in the market.


Your backtest may assume you bought at EGP 10.00. Live, you might fill at EGP 10.03, EGP 10.05, or not fill at all. The exact number is unknowable ahead of time, so you use a realistic buffer.

A serious backtest does not ask, “What if every order fills perfectly?”

It asks, “Does this idea still work after realistic costs and imperfect execution?”

This matters especially in markets where liquidity differs sharply from one stock to another. A liquid name and a thinly traded name can have completely different execution realities. Same strategy. Same chart. Very different fills.

06 — DISTORTIONS

Where Backtests Get Distorted

The point is not to fear backtests. The point is to know where the mirror bends.

D1

Look-Ahead Bias

Using information in the backtest that you would not have had at the time of the trade. Example: entering a trade at 10:00am using a closing price that only became known at the end of the day.

D2

Survivorship Bias

Testing only on stocks that still exist today. That quietly removes companies that were delisted, suspended, or failed — which makes the historical market look cleaner than it really was.

D3

Overfitting

Tuning a strategy until it fits one specific historical period beautifully. The backtest improves, but the idea becomes less real. You are no longer discovering a market behavior. You are sculpting the strategy around noise.

D4

Ignoring Costs and Slippage

A strategy that makes money only before commission, fees, spreads, and imperfect fills is not a strategy yet. It is a clean chart with messy reality removed.

07 — EXECUTION ASSUMPTIONS

Limit Orders and Missed Fills

A backtest should match the way you expect to execute live.

In live algo trading, limit orders are often preferred because they give price certainty. You decide the maximum price you are willing to pay, or the minimum price you are willing to sell at. That makes live execution closer to the assumptions in your backtest.

The Benefit

Price control. A limit order protects you from getting filled far away from the price your strategy expected.

The Tradeoff

Missed fills. If the market never reaches your limit price, the trade does not happen. A backtest that assumes every limit order fills is pretending away the main tradeoff.

The practical solution is not to pretend fills are perfect. It is to widen your slippage assumptions, model missed trades where appropriate, and keep the backtest conservative enough that live execution has room to be imperfect.

↳ The cleaner the execution assumption, the more suspicious you should be. Real markets are never that polite.
08 — THE RIGHT CONCLUSION

A Backtest Is a Hypothesis, Not a Promise

The goal is not certainty. The goal is a better filter.

A bad backtest says, “This made money in the past, so it will make money live.” That is the wrong conclusion.

A good backtest says, “This idea behaved coherently in historical conditions, survived realistic costs, avoided obvious distortions, and is worth testing more seriously.” That is a much stronger way to think.

Backtesting is how builders turn trading ideas into testable prototypes. It does not remove uncertainty. It makes the uncertainty visible.
09 — SUMMARY

What You Should Now Understand

Six things to carry into live execution.

Key Takeaways — Part 04
  • A backtest runs a defined strategy through historical data to simulate how it would have behaved.
  • The result is not just return. You should read the full behavior: drawdown, win rate, payoff, trade count, exposure, and costs.
  • Treat historical data like ML data: train on one portion, test on another, and keep recent data untouched as a reality check.
  • Commission is known and should be built in exactly. Slippage is uncertain and should be modeled conservatively.
  • Backtests get distorted by look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, overfitting, and unrealistic execution assumptions.
  • A backtest is a hypothesis, not a promise. It tells you whether an idea is worth testing more seriously.
