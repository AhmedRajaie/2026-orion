01 — DEFINITIONS

The Word Does a Lot of Damage

Strip out the mystique and you find something very simple.

"Algorithmic trading" sounds like something done in a glass tower by people with PhDs and access to fiber-optic cables that mortals don't have. The word algorithm gets used to mean "complicated math that I can't follow." That framing has kept a lot of curious people out of a field they'd be perfectly capable of understanding.

An algorithm is a defined set of rules, applied the same way every time, to produce a predictable result. A recipe is an algorithm. A traffic light is an algorithm. If the light is red, stop. If it turns green, go. That's it.

Algorithmic trading is the same idea applied to buying and selling: a set of rules for when to enter a trade, when to exit, and how much to commit — applied mechanically, without emotion, without exceptions.

↳ That's the entire concept. Everything else in this series is detail.

The barrier to entry isn't intelligence. It's clarity. The moment you can express your trading idea as a rule with no ambiguity, you have an algo. Most people who think they're discretionary traders are actually running fuzzy, half-articulated algorithms in their head — they just don't know it. "I buy stocks when they look oversold" is an algorithm with the parameters left blank. Writing it down is the work of becoming a better trader, whether or not you ever automate it.

Card 1 of 5 — The Simplest Rule

One condition.
One action.

A single condition triggers a single action. Anyone can understand it. Anyone could follow it manually — if they were watching the screen at the exact second it happened, which they won't be. The computer is.

This is where every algo starts. Simple, testable, unambiguous.

If the price of CIB drops by 5% from yesterday's close, buy 100 shares.
Entry condition · Instrument · Quantity
Card 2 of 5 — A Trailing Stop-Loss

The exit point
moves with the trade.

If CIB rises to 90, the stop-loss rises to 85.5. If it then rises to 100, the stop rises to 95. The stop only moves up, never down.

This is impossible to do well manually. You'd have to recalculate across every position you hold, all day. A computer does it on every price update without complaint. This is what people mean when they say algos can "let winners run."

Buy CIB at EGP 80. Set a stop-loss 5% below the highest price the stock has reached since I bought it.
Entry · Trailing exit · Automatic recalculation
Card 3 of 5 — Scaling In Over Time

Split a large order
into small pieces.

Splitting a large order is one of the oldest reasons algos exist. Large buys move prices against you — every share you buy gets a little more expensive as the market notices. Spreading the order out reduces that impact.

Institutional desks do this constantly. You can too, with five lines of code.

I want to buy 1,000 shares of COMI, but I don't want to move the price. Buy 100 shares every 15 minutes between 10am and 12:30pm.
TWAP execution · Market impact reduction
Card 4 of 5 — Watching the Whole Market

Coverage
as an edge.

A human watches five stocks. A computer watches the entire exchange. The algo here isn't even placing trades — it's surfacing opportunities you'd miss because you can't be everywhere at once.

Even if your trading is fully discretionary, an algo can be your eyes.

Every minute, scan all EGX stocks. Alert me when any stock crosses above its 50-day moving average on volume at least double its 20-day average.
Market scan · Signal detection · No trades placed
Card 5 of 5 — A Complete Strategy

Five rules.
A full trading system.

This is what an actual deployable strategy looks like — and it's still not complicated. Each rule is specific, testable, executable. Together they define a complete system that could run unattended for months.

You don't need a PhD for this. You need clarity.

↳ Each of these five algos is doable in under 50 lines of code. None requires AI. All are out of reach manually — not because they're complex, but because they need speed or coverage that humans don't have.

For EGX stocks with price > EGP 5 and 30-day avg volume > 100k shares:

— Enter long when the 20-day MA crosses above the 50-day MA.
— Position size: 2% of portfolio.
— Stop-loss: 8% below entry, trailing.
— Take-profit: when 20-day crosses back below 50-day.
— Max concurrent positions: 10.
Entry · Sizing · Stop · Exit · Portfolio limits

02 — THE SHIFT

What Changes When a Computer Runs It

The same rules apply. What changes is who's pressing the button — and that one change cascades into things that matter.

Speed
A computer can evaluate the rule and place the order in milliseconds. You can't — and milliseconds are the margin between hitting your price and missing it.
Consistency
The computer applies the rule the same way at 9am and at 2pm, on a good day and a bad one. You won't — emotion bends rules in the moment.
Coverage
A computer can monitor every stock on EGX simultaneously, in real time. You can watch maybe five. Coverage at scale is structurally unavailable to a human.
No Emotion
The computer doesn't get scared, greedy, or tired. It doesn't second-guess the rule because it had a bad morning. We'll spend the next part on why this is the one that matters most.
↳ None of this requires AI. None of it requires advanced math. It requires defined rules.
03 — IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

Algo Trading Is Not AI Trading

The words are used interchangeably and they shouldn't be. The distinction matters for how you build and debug your systems.

Algorithmic Trading
Rules-based execution, defined by humans. Has existed since long before AI. Practiced on paper in the 1970s, in spreadsheets in the 1980s, and in code from the 1990s onward. The rules were always written by people.
AI Trading
An extension of algo trading, not a replacement. AI can be layered in to read news, interpret unstructured data, or adjust parameters over time. But the frame is still algorithmic. The AI lives inside a system of rules — it doesn't replace them.
The progression is: rules-based algo first → AI-augmented algo later.

Skipping the first step is how you end up with a complicated system you don't understand and can't debug. Most professional algos still run on rules alone. We'll cover what AI brings in Part 7. For the next five parts, set AI aside completely.

05 — SUMMARY

What You Should Now Understand

Five things to take away from Part 1 before moving on.

Key Takeaways — Part 01
  • An algorithm is a defined set of rules applied consistently — no more, no less. A recipe is an algorithm. A traffic light is an algorithm.
  • Algorithmic trading applies the same idea to buying and selling: rules for entry, exit, and sizing — executed mechanically, without exception.
  • The barrier isn't intelligence or math. It's the discipline of writing your rules down with no ambiguity. Clarity is the work.
  • Algo trading is not AI trading. AI is a later layer. Rules come first. Most professional algos still run without a single line of machine learning.
  • What a computer adds is speed, consistency, coverage, and emotional flatness — not magic. The rules can be as simple as the CIB example above.
